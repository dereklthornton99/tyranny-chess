Tyranny Chess

Ordinary chess with one addition: the king may command the execution of his own citizens. Same geometry, same board, same objective — but any piece standing in the crown’s way can be removed, and the executioner is rarely the king himself.

Reading the board

Dot — an ordinary move to an empty square.

Red ring — capture an enemy piece. Written Nxd5.

Brass brackets — capture your own piece. Written N⊗d2.

A piece is selected on click. If you then click one of your own pieces that is bracketed, it is captured — press Esc to deselect instead.

Where the rule actually lands

The king
You may never capture your own king. Everything else — check, mate, legality — assumes each side has exactly one, so this is the single hard exclusion.
Pawns
A pawn takes its own pieces diagonally only, exactly as it takes enemies. Its forward push is still blocked by anything in front of it — so a pawn can never take the pawn directly ahead of it, only a friendly piece on an adjacent file.
Castling
Unchanged — the traditional move works exactly as it always did, and no restriction on it is lifted. Two knock-on effects: executing your own rook forfeits that side’s castling right, and a piece blocking f1/g1 now has more squares to vacate to, since it may leave by taking a friendly piece. Executing the blocker itself does not help — your capturer simply lands on the square it was standing on.
En passant
Unreachable against your own pawn. The capture window is one ply wide and always belongs to the opponent, so it needs no special rule.
Checkmate
Strictly harder. The new escape is the king executing its own piece to reach a flight square. Blocking is not a new resource — a friendly piece already on the check line means there was no check. Swept 1,669 positions in check: all 5,654 self-capture escapes were king moves, none an interposition. Every mate here is also a mate in standard chess; the reverse is not true.
Stalemate
Rarer by accident — almost any position with two friendly pieces in capture geometry has a legal move. But it becomes reachable on purpose, which standard chess cannot do: a lone extra piece is often the only thing keeping a doomed side obliged to move, and here the king can eat it and offer the stalemate. Offer, not claim — it still needs the opponent to walk in, so it is a swindle against people and a blunder against a searching engine.
The floor
You cannot delete your way to nothing. A capture leaves the capturer standing, so ordinary self-captures bottom out at king plus one piece. Only the king can remove that last piece — shedding your final man is a king-only act, and it has to land on a square the king could safely occupy anyway.
